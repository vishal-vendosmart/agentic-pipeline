## Post 1
The founder opens a project variance report on a tablet in the boardroom.
The line for the main conveyor frame shows a loss of $18,000.
The founder asks why the cost jumped.
The project manager points to a single cell in a shared spreadsheet.
The price came from a 2017 purchase order.
It was a scanned PDF with a faded red stamp.

The document is a grainy scan.
The ink has bled into the paper.
The estimator can barely make out the part number.
They open three different legacy folders in OneDrive.
They search for the same part in a project from 2015.
They find a different price.
They spend an hour trying to understand why the 2017 price is lower.
They find a handwritten note in the margin.
It says special discount for volume.

The discount that won the bid in 2017 is the loss that shows up on the P&L in 2026. The PDF is the only witness.

If you have ever seen a project profit evaporate because of a ten year old PDF, you are not alone.

## Post 2
The founder is reviewing a customer change order for an additional $45,000.
The client is asking for a feature they claim was missed.
The founder opens the original technical specification.
It is a 70-page PDF with dense paragraphs and no index.

On page 42, there is a requirement for a reinforced base.
The proposal engineer matched this to a standard base in the BoQ.
They spent three hours auditing the document.
They cross-referenced a 2021 project folder.
They found a part called a support plate and used that price.
The terminology in the spec was slightly different from the terminology in the archive.
The human failed to catch the semantic gap.
The gap is $45,000.

The system asks the engineer to be a perfect semantic matching engine.
They fight with unstructured data and inconsistent naming.
The result is a bid that looks accurate but hides a massive hole.
The company accepts the bid and signs the contract.

Both sides were right. The spec said reinforced base. The archive said standard base. The contract signed on the average.

The most dangerous part of a bid is the word that means two different things.

## Post 3
The founder sits in the back of a car as an email arrives from a long-time customer.
The company missed the bid window for a $3M packaging line.
The window was five days.
The estimation took eleven.

The founder calls the proposal lead.
The lead explains that the senior estimator was stuck in the archives.
They were hunting for a historical price for a custom valve.
They had to open 50 different folders in OneDrive.
Most were named with obscure project codes like PRJ-2019-B.
They were manually deduping vendor quotes from 2019 and 2022.
They were checking a Slack DM from a project manager who left three years ago.
The DM mentioned a pricing shift that was never recorded in the ERP.

The process is human-intensive and meticulous.
The estimator is the only person who can bridge the gap between a PDF and a bid.
They do it because the system has no other way to find the truth.
The shop loses millions because the data is trapped in fragments.

The company missed the bid because the estimator was the slowest part of the process. The rate limiter is a person with a keyboard.

Who in your shop is the only person who can find a price, and what happens to your growth when they are overwhelmed?

## Post 4
The founder is staring at the quarterly P&L, where the gross margin is only 4 percent.
The target was 18 percent.
The founder looks at the top five projects.
Every one of them had a margin leak during the proposal phase.

In my experience, these leaks are not the result of bad judgment.
They are the result of manual normalization.
An estimator pulls a price from a scanned invoice.
They adjust it for inflation.
They match it to a part that is almost the same.
They do this for 400 lines in a BoQ.
They check a mystery Excel sheet.
They verify the part against a blurry JPG.
The errors are small.
$200 here.
$500 there.
The cumulative effect is a disaster.

The margin is the difference between the number in the ERP and the number in the bid. The estimator produces the second number using the first one and a prayer.

The company calls this a pricing error.
It is a data failure.
