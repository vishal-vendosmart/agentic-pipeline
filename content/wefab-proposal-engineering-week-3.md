## Post 1
The design lead sits in the bid review meeting.
The room is small. The air is stale.
The cost estimator projects a BoQ onto the wall.
Line 84 is a custom mounting arm for $2,400.

The design lead knows this arm is more complex than the one from the 2017 project.
The estimator tells them the price is based on a historical project from a OneDrive folder.
The design lead leans in to see the pixels on the screen.
They open the source.
It is a scanned PDF of a purchase order from nine years ago.
The estimator scrolls through the document, pausing at a blurred signature in the footer.
The part number is slightly different.
The description simply says support bracket.

The design lead spends twenty minutes performing semantic matching.
They compare the old drawing to the new requirements.
They check a legacy ERP field to see if the material grade changed.
They realize the estimator matched a bracket to an arm.
The estimator matched a bracket to an arm because the spreadsheet demanded a number and the archive had nothing better.
They did it because the bid was due on Monday.

The bid is two days out. The person who knows what the part actually looks like is being asked to confirm a number from 2017.

If you have ever spent a bid review arguing over whether a bracket is actually an arm, you are not alone.

## Post 2
The design lead is reviewing the final proposal package.
They open the CAD drawing for the main assembly.
The drawing shows four specialized sensors.
They open the BoQ.
The estimator has listed one sensor for $1,200.

The design lead looks for the source of the price.
It is a vendor quote from 2020.
The original project was for a smaller machine.
That project only needed one sensor.
The estimator performed a manual match based on the part name.
They did not verify the quantity against the new design.

The design lead opens a separate Excel sheet.
The folder structure is a maze of dates and acronyms.
They click through four levels of sub-folders.
They search for other historical prices for this sensor.
They find three different entries in an old project folder.
One is in a PDF.
One is a handwritten note in a JPG with a smeared ink blot over the currency symbol.
One is a Slack message from a former engineer who left the company years ago.
The prices vary by $300 each.

One sensor on the drawing. One sensor on the BoQ. The estimator matched the name and missed the count. The error will live in the bid forever.

The most expensive error in a bid is a unit count that matches a name but not a drawing.

## Post 3
The design lead sits in their home office, illuminated by the blue light of a monitor.
The bid for a packaging line is due tomorrow.
The sales lead sends a message.
They need a technical sign-off on a $15,000 sub-assembly cost.

The design lead looks at the cost estimate.
The price comes from a 2021 project.
They search the company OneDrive for the original vendor RFQ.
They find a chain of twelve emails.
The vendor quote was valid for thirty days.
The estimator used that price as a benchmark for 2026.

The design lead tries to normalize the price for five years of inflation.
They search for a more recent invoice in the ERP.
The ERP field is empty.
They find a WhatsApp screenshot from a project manager.
The screenshot is blurry and cropped, sent from a phone with a cracked screen.
The image shows a price increase from last quarter.
The design lead manually updates the BoQ.
They are fighting with unstructured data while the clock runs down.

It is Sunday night. The design lead is the only person in the company willing to validate a five year old number with a blurry screenshot from a cracked phone.

Who in your shop is the only person capable of validating a five year old price in the middle of a Sunday night?

## Post 4
The bid was won, and the design lead starts the actual engineering.
They request the first long-lead part from the vendor.
The vendor sends a quote for $9,200.
The BoQ estimate was $6,500.

The design lead traces the $6,500 back to the source.
It was a scanned invoice from 2019.
The invoice was for Grade 304 stainless steel.
The new project requires Grade 316.
The estimator matched the parts by name.
They did not see the material grade in the grainy PDF.

In my experience, the win is the moment the data failure becomes a financial loss.
The estimator matched semantic meanings across a PDF and a spreadsheet with no other tool available.
The result was a precision match of the wrong part.

The gap is $2,700 for a single part.
It seems small until you multiply it across the entire machine.
The project manager does not look up from his laptop when the design lead explains the error.

The bid was won on a number that was never true. The PDF was too blurry to see the difference between two grades of steel. The project manager will hear about it on Monday.

The company calls this a budget overrun.
It is a data failure.
