## Post 4
Pillar: B (Sales workflow #6 — Building the proposal package)
Date: Mon Jul 27
Close Shape: A (Shared experience)
Writer Note: Hook lands the proposal engineer's experience. Mechanism is the format-heavy day, not the design day. Engineer positioned as victim of the system.

A proposal engineer at a mid-market custom shop spends three days building a proposal package.
Two of those three days are formatting.

I have sat with proposal engineers and watched the week split. Day one is understanding what the customer actually needs. Day two and three are copying numbers into a template, chasing the right revision of a drawing, getting sign-off from three different leads.

The work is meticulous. The proposal engineer knows the customer will judge the company by the document. They know a wrong number ends the deal. They do the work carefully.

The system has no template that updates itself. The system has no place where the latest revision of a drawing is automatically the right one. The proposal engineer carries the gaps the system should have closed.

The cost is not the days. The cost is the deals the proposal engineer never got to write because they were reformatting the last one.

If you have ever rebuilt the same proposal three times in a month because the template changed, you are not alone.

## Post 5
Pillar: B (Sales workflow #6 — Answering the RFQ)
Date: Wed Jul 29
Close Shape: D (Reframing)
Writer Note: Hook names the RFQ as a moment of ownership ambiguity. Mechanism: the deliverable has no owner, so it sits in two inboxes. Reframing close names the real problem.

The RFQ came in on Monday.
By Friday, the proposal engineer is still waiting for the cost estimate. The customer has asked twice.

I have watched this from the sales side. The proposal engineer is doing the work. The cost estimator is doing the work. Neither owns the deliverable. The system has no rule for who delivers the RFQ response.

The RFQ sits in two inboxes. The proposal engineer thinks the cost estimator has it. The cost estimator thinks the proposal engineer has it. The customer stops asking. The deal goes quiet.

The company calls this a coordination problem. It is not a coordination problem. It is a workflow with no owner. The same RFQ in a different shop would have one name on it. The name would be the deadline.

The bottleneck is not the people. The bottleneck is the system that does not assign the work.

## Post 7
Pillar: A (Workflow #4 — Creating technical documents)
Date: Mon Aug 3
Close Shape: A (Shared experience)
Writer Note: Hook names the technical document as the senior engineer's responsibility. Mechanism: the senior name on the document, but the work is formatting. Engineer as victim.

A technical document. Forty-seven pages of spec.
A senior engineer formats it for three days. The customer is waiting.

I have seen this in process equipment and energy equipment shops. The senior engineer's name is on the document because the company needs a senior engineer's name on the document. The formatting is meticulous. The judgment is small.

The work is real. The document has to ship. The customer cannot review without it. The senior engineer knows this and stays on it.

But the senior engineer is also the person the company trusts with the new product line. The senior engineer is also the person who can solve the design problem waiting on their desk. The system uses the senior engineer for the document because the system has no template, no tool, no junior owner for the document. The senior engineer is the safe choice.

The cost is the design problem that ships a week late because the document had to ship first.

If you have ever spent your week formatting the document instead of solving the problem, you are not alone.

## Post 8
Pillar: A (Workflow #5 — Extracting BOMs from legacy files)
Date: Wed Aug 5
Close Shape: C (Open question)
Writer Note: Hook names the parsing task. Mechanism: the engineer is doing a job a machine could read. The output is the same format either way. Question close names the cost.

A wiring diagram from 2012. A forty-seven-page PDF.
A junior engineer typing part numbers into a spreadsheet for two days.

I have watched this in industrial automation shops. The diagram is correct. The reading is manual. The output is a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet is the same format a machine could have read.

The junior engineer types carefully. The senior engineer reviews every line. The cost estimator waits for the BOM. The proposal engineer cannot start the quote.

The work has to ship. The work is meticulous. The system has no tool to read the diagram. The system uses the engineer to do the parsing. The engineer's salary is the parsing cost.

The diagram is from a machine the company has built for fifteen years. The same parsing will happen on the next project. And the one after that. The system will keep paying engineer-hours to read what the machine could.

Who in your shop is the person everyone waits for when a legacy file has to be parsed by hand?

## Post 9
Pillar: E (Talent/attrition — The engineer who quits in month 14)
Date: Fri Aug 7
Close Shape: D (Reframing)
Writer Note: Hook names the average tenure. Mechanism: the engineer joined to design, the system gave them rework, the engineer left. Reframing close names the cost vs. the diagnosis.

Fourteen months. That is the average tenure of the senior engineer who joins a custom shop to do great work and leaves because the system gave them rework.

I have watched this in three companies in the last year. The engineer joins to design. By month fourteen, the design is the third thing on their list. The first thing is rework. The second thing is documentation. The design waits.

The engineer does not complain. The engineer is not difficult. The engineer simply looks at the work and sees the next ten years. The same ten years. The same rework. The same documentation. The design that never gets the week it needs.

The engineer leaves. The company posts the role again. The replacement takes another fourteen months to reach the same frustration.

The company calls this turnover. The company is looking at the wrong number. The number is not the months of tenure. The number is the years of training the company invested in the person who is now working somewhere else.

The cost is not the recruiter fee. The cost is the design the company did not get.

## Post 10
Pillar: B (Cost of slow quotes — The 11-day quote)
Date: Mon Aug 10
Close Shape: B (Quiet observation)
Writer Note: Hook names the actual number (11 days) and the moment the deal was gone. Mechanism: the work was right, the system was slow. Quiet observation close lands the misallocation.

Eleven days. That is what the cost estimate took.
The customer had moved on by day six.

I have watched this in SPM and process equipment shops. The cost estimator pulled data from twelve places to build one number. The number was right. The system was slow. The deal was gone.

The cost estimator did the work. The work was meticulous. The customer needed an answer on day three. The answer came on day eleven.

The company paid the salary for the eleven days. The company got no order. The next month, the same shop will quote another customer in eleven days. The next month, the same customer will sign with the shop that quoted in six hours.

The cost is not the salary. The cost is the eleven days the system made the work take.

The most expensive number in custom manufacturing is the one that arrived after the deal was already gone.

The shop that lost the deal was not slower at engineering.

The competitor quoted in six hours. The shop that lost took eleven days. The customer did not compare the engineering. The customer compared the speed.

I have watched this in packaging, SPM, and process equipment. The shop that won was not better. The shop that won was faster. The shop that won had built a system that moved the work. The shop that lost had not.

The customer did not wait eleven days because the engineer was slow. The customer did not wait eleven days because the estimate was hard. The customer waited eleven days because the system had a queue.

The shop that lost will tell the sales team to call the customer earlier. The sales team already called. The shop that won did not call faster. The shop that won delivered faster.

What does your customer's first six hours with your shop look like?

## Post 12
Pillar: D (ICP definition — The 60-engineer shop)
Date: Fri Aug 14
Close Shape: D (Reframing)
Writer Note: Hook names the size and the visible inconsistency. Mechanism: the engineers want consistency, the company never built the system. Reframing close names the real diagnosis.

Sixty engineers. Every proposal looks different.
The customer notices. The customer assumes the machine will be the same.

I have sat in leadership reviews of shops like this. The proposal formats are different because the engineers built their own. The drawing standards are different because no one wrote them down. The BOMs are different because the templates are different.

The engineers are not the problem. The engineers want consistency. The engineers would prefer a shared standard. The company never built the system that would give it to them.

The customer reads the first proposal and sees the inconsistency. The customer assumes the machine will be the same. The customer asks the competitor for a quote. The competitor's first proposal looks the same as the competitor's last proposal.

The company calls this a sales problem. It is not a sales problem. It is a system the company never built.

The shop that wins the next deal will be the shop whose first document already looks like the second.
