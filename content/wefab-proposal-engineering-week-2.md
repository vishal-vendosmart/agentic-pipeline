## Post 1
The proposal engineer is mapping a Bill of Quantities to a technical specification.
The spec is a 60-page PDF with a dozen different fonts.
The BoQ is a spreadsheet with 300 lines of raw parts.
The engineer must ensure every line in the BoQ has a corresponding requirement in the spec.

The estimator listed a part as a stainless steel bracket.
The spec calls it a reinforced mounting plate.
The engineer searches for the part number in the archive.
They find a project from 2022 in a folder named Project-X-Final-v2.
In that folder, the part was listed as a support angle.

The engineer spends three hours performing semantic matching.
They verify that a bracket and a plate and an angle are the same physical part.
They check a scanned vendor invoice from 2021 to be sure.
The invoice is a blurry JPG with a coffee stain on the total.
They manually cross-reference the dimensions across three different formats.
They match a 12mm hole on a drawing to a 0.47 inch callout in a text file.

Three documents. One part. The engineer is the only one who can convince the system they are the same thing.

If you have ever spent a Monday night mapping a BoQ to a blurry JPG, you are not alone.

## Post 2
The proposal engineer finds a $12,000 lump sum for miscellaneous hardware in the cost estimate.
The customer demands a detailed breakdown of every bolt and washer.

The engineer opens a project folder from 2019.
They hunt for the actual purchase orders.
The orders are scattered across five different OneDrive folders.
One is a PDF with no searchable text.
One is a handwritten note scanned into a JPG.
One is a series of emails from a vendor who went out of business in 2022.

The engineer manually extracts the unit prices.
They open a calculator.
They normalize the 2019 prices for 2026 inflation.
They check three different material indices to see if the jump is consistent.
They build a new table to prove the $12,000 is accurate.
The process takes an entire afternoon of staring at spreadsheets.
The engineer is the only person who knows where the ghosts of old projects live.

The customer asked for a breakdown. The engineer spent the afternoon building one out of five different archives. The $12,000 is the answer. The work is the proof.

The most expensive way to find a price is to ask the person who remembers it.

## Post 3
The proposal engineer is four hours away from the bid deadline.
The sales lead sends an email.
The customer wants a change in the material for the main frame.
They want titanium instead of steel.

The engineer opens the proposal slide deck.
They find twelve different mentions of the material.
They open the updated BoQ.
The estimator has changed the price for the metal grade.
The engineer must now manually sync this change across the entire technical package.

They update the slides.
They update the compliance matrix.
They update the technical datasheet.
One mistake in one slide creates a legal liability.
The engineer performs a manual semantic audit.
They read every line of the 40-page proposal.
They ensure the new material is consistent in every single paragraph.
They check the footnotes.
They check the appendix.

The audit is not quality control. It is the only thing standing between the bid and a typo that costs the company a lawsuit.

Who in your shop is the only person capable of auditing a 40-page proposal in three hours without a single error?

## Post 4
The proposal engineer is handing the won bid over to the project manager.
The project manager opens the internal cost sheet.
They see a $5,000 line for a specialty valve.

The project manager asks where that price came from.
The engineer traces it back.
They find a scanned PDF from a vendor in 2020.
The price was for a volume of ten units.
The current bid is for only one unit.
The inflation adjustment was based on a generic index.
The actual cost today is $8,500.

In my experience, the win is often the moment the real risk begins.
The proposal engineer spent weeks matching unstructured data.
They used scanned invoices and old emails.
They did their best to normalize the data.
The system gave them no other tool.
They were asked to be a database and a calculator and a historian.

The bid is won. The number is wrong. The signature on the contract is the same one that signed off on the estimate.

The company calls this a win.
It is a margin leak.
