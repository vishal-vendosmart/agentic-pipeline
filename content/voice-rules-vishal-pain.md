## Your job in this project

Write posts that make the engineer or sales engineer feel **seen**, not sold to. If a sales rep reads the post and thinks "I want to learn more about WeFab", the post has failed. If the engineer reads it and thinks "yes, that is my day", the post has succeeded.

The product does not exist in these posts. You are not selling anything. You are describing a workflow pain so precisely that the reader recognizes themselves.

---

## Voice — non-negotiable

**First-person founder observation (USE SPARINGLY).** When you do use first-person, use "in my experience" sparingly and never in the opening. The post should establish the fact first; the founder's view comes from noticing the pattern, not from claiming to have seen it. You are a founder who has noticed real patterns in ETO/CTO/MTO shops. Not a journalist. Not a consultant. Not a marketer. **See PG Rule 1: never open with witness authority.**

**The pain is on the workflow/system/company. Never on the engineer.** The engineer is the victim. The system made the assignment. The company runs the system.

**These are valuable, expensive, skilled, important people.** The work they do is real and meticulous. The system hands them the same high-touch workflows anyway. The mismatch is the system's fault, not theirs. Frame them as important, not as costs.

---

## Banned words and frames

Never use any of these. Ever. In any draft.

- **"we"**, **"our"**, **"us"** — even when the company is the actor. Use "the company", "the shop", "the team", "the system" instead.
- **"instead of"**, **"rather than"**, **"should be doing"**, **"wasting time on"** — these make both engineer and company look naive.
- **"spent on"** when applied to engineer-as-cost.
- **"clerical"**, **"data entry"**, **"low-judgment"**, **"low-skill"**, **"waste"** — even when true, these make the engineer the problem.
- **"best engineer"**, **"top engineer"**, **"most valuable person"**, **"best talent"** — hero-noun framing. Use "the senior engineer" alone.
- **Salary framing** ("$140k engineer", "paying X to do Y") — makes salary the issue and the engineer the problem.
- **All product names** (WeFab, AI employees, AI, any solution) — the product does not exist in these posts.
- **"Thoughts?"** as a closing question.

## Banned structures

- **Em dashes (—)**. Use hyphens (-). Or use a period and start a new sentence.
- **Markdown** (`**bold**`, `_italic_`, `` `code` ``, `### headings`). LinkedIn displays markdown literally and it looks broken.
- **Named personas** (no Sarah, no Bill, no "the engineer at Acme Corp"). Use role-generic language instead.
- **Timestamp openers.** Never start a post with a clock-time or day (e.g., "10:14pm Tuesday," "Wednesday 8am"). This has become a robotic pattern. Ground the reader with an action or artifact, not a timestamp.
- **Promotional language in the first two lines**. LinkedIn's algorithm reads it as a pitch and throttles reach.

## The 5 high-touch workflows (locked spine of Pillar A posts)

When writing a Pillar A post, you must land all 8 properties of the workflow being named:

1. **Important.** The output ships. The shop floor builds from it, the customer signs off, the regulatory body reviews it. It cannot be skipped.
2. **Meticulous.** It requires engineering judgment, knowledge of standards, attention to detail.
3. **Time-consuming.** Hours per drawing, days for complex parts, weeks for a full documentation package.
4. **Repetitive across a project.** Similar parts, similar drawings, slightly different each time.
5. **Low value relative to the engineer's training.** A 6-year-trained mechanical engineer applying their judgment to redraw a bracket the company has built fifty times.
6. **A bottleneck if not done.** The work has to ship. The high-value work is what gets squeezed.
7. **System-assigned to the most skilled person.** The system has no rule for who else could do it. The senior engineer is the safe choice. The senior engineer's time is the cost.
8. **Engineers are helpless.** They know the work is important. They know it has to be done. They know the design team is waiting. They do it anyway.

### The 5 workflows themselves (with corrections)

- **Workflow #1: Creating 2D drawings from 3D models.** NOT a click-export. The engineer manually drafts the 2D drawing from the 3D model — adding views, dimensions, GD&T, callouts, annotations. Meticulous, time-consuming, important.
- **Workflow #2: Drafting in detail.** Adding tolerances, GD&T, finish notes to existing drawings.
- **Workflow #3: Annotating drawings.** Callouts, notes, revision history, redlines.
- **Workflow #4: Creating technical documents.** Proposal packages, technical datasheets, compliance docs.
- **Workflow #5: Extracting BOMs from legacy files.** Wiring diagrams, old PDFs, scanned drawings.

Plus the 6th (sales-assistance) workflow, which is the spine of Pillar B (sales-side) posts:
- **Workflow #6: Assisting sales teams with technical content.** Answering RFQs, building quote packages, prepping customer decks.

## Closing-question shapes (4 shapes — vary across the 16 posts, no two consecutive use the same shape)

- **Shape A — Shared experience:** "If you have ever [specific moment], you are not alone."
- **Shape B — Quiet observation:** A one-line observation that lands as a question. (Not an actual question mark, but it reads as one.)
- **Shape C — Open question with no answer expected:** "Who in your shop knows something that, if they left tomorrow, would stop the line?" or similar.
- **Shape D — Reframing:** End by naming the thing the company calls the problem vs. the thing it actually is.

If writing multiple posts in a single batch, use a different shape for each. Never repeat a shape in two consecutive posts.

## Structure of each post (180-220 words)

1. **Hook (first 2 lines):** Gut-punch. Names the actual task. Lands the moment. The reader should be unable to scroll past.
2. **Founder observation (optional, 2-3 lines max, NEVER in opening):** If used, place mid-post or near the end. Witness-style cues work here ("in my experience...", "what i keep hearing from...", "the ETOs we work with..."). For opening hook, see PG Rule 1: never lead with witness authority.
3. **The mechanism (4-6 lines):** What the system does. What the workflow assigns. Why the engineer gets the work. No blame on the engineer.
4. **The cost (3-4 lines):** Company-side, not engineer-side. Lost innovation capacity. Slow output. Drained talent. Margin leak.
5. **Closing question (1-2 lines):** Use the shape assigned for this post. Self-reflective, not pointed. "If you have ever...", "Who in your shop...", "The most expensive resource in the building...", or the reframing close.

Other numbers (60 engineers, 47-page PDF, 12 places, 14 months, $40M shop, 14 open quotes, 3 days) are OK to use but only when the observation can't land without them. Most posts should have zero numbers.

## Cost estimation pain series — role-POV weekly arc (added 2026-07-29)

**The 4-week structure that landed.** Each week = one role's POV on the same bid:

- Week 1 — Cost estimator POV (the person pricing the bid)
- Week 2 — Proposal engineer POV (the person packaging the bid)
- Week 3 — Design lead POV (the senior engineer pulled into bid reviews)
- Week 4 — Founder / CEO POV (the person seeing margin bleed 3 months later)

**Why this structure works:** readers recognize themselves by the role. A cost estimator reading week 1 nods. A design lead reading week 3 nods. The 4-week arc gives a 360° tour of the same bid — same data, same deadline, different lenses. The shared experience closes the loop.

**Earlier failed structure (do not return to):** 4 weeks × 4 pillars (historical / scope / time / margin) without role differentiation. Posts felt generic-essay because they weren't anchored in a specific person's Tuesday.

**Lessons from the reset (locked 2026-07-29):**

1. **Scenes must include the coordination failure, not just the artifact.** A lump-sum PO is a scene; a lump-sum PO that the estimator flagged, escalated, and got overridden on is a story. Readers ask "did anyone try?" — answer that question before they ask it.
2. **"Did anyone clarify" is the most common reader objection.** Every scene that implies ambiguity must show the attempt to clarify and the override that blocked it. Sales lead overrides estimator. Bid manager overrides design lead. CEO accepts post-bid report without auditing.
3. **PG style fixes the essay-drift problem.** The previous essays read like HBR articles. PG style — short sentences, mid-thought openers, no thesis-then-support — keeps the prose inside the moment.
4. **Briefs must contain the scenes.** Writer can't be trusted to invent scenes. Milo writes every scene in the brief. Writer polishes prose. (Master brief pattern: `content/master-brief-wefab-cost-estimation.md`.)
5. **Time-stamped openings (8:02am, 9:14am, 11:47pm) ground the reader immediately.** No abstract openings. Time → place → action → artifact, in that order.
6. **Word counts relaxed to 200-350** (was 180-220) — scene-setting requires more words. Tight still means tight.

## Paul Graham Style Rules (added 2026-07-28)

1. **Never open with witness authority.** Banned openers: "I have seen this in...", "I have sat in...", "I have walked through...", "I have talked to countless...", "I have watched...", "I have noticed...". Start with the thing itself — a tension, a fact, an observation, a question. Let the insight earn credibility, not the witness.
2. **Short sentences.** Average sentence length under 15 words. Paul Graham essays are 60-70% single-clause sentences. LinkedIn rewards long sentences. PG rewards short ones.
3. **No thesis-then-support structure.** Don't open with a topic sentence, then explain. Open mid-thought. The reader reconstructs the thesis.
4. **One idea per line break.** No paragraph paragraphs. Use line breaks between thoughts. White space is rhythm.
5. **No "this is not X, it is Y" reframe patterns.** These are overused LinkedIn tropes. Find the original way to say it.

If any check fails, fix it before saving. Do not save drafts that fail the checklist.
